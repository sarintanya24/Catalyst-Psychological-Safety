## 2. The SCARF Model

### Overview

The **SCARF Model** is David Rock's framework for understanding the five domains of social experience that the brain treats with the same intensity as survival needs like food and water. First published in 2008 in the paper *"SCARF: A Brain-Based Model for Collaborating With and Influencing Others"* (NeuroLeadership Journal, Volume 1, pp. 1-9), SCARF stands for:

- **S** -- Status
- **C** -- Certainty
- **A** -- Autonomy
- **R** -- Relatedness
- **F** -- Fairness

### The Neuroscience Foundation

The brain's overarching organizing principle is to **minimize danger and maximize reward**. Neuroscientist Evian Gordon called this "the fundamental organizing principle of the brain." This applies to social interactions just as powerfully as to physical survival:

**The Threat Response Mechanism**:
- When a SCARF domain is **threatened**, the brain activates a **threat response** (fight/flight/freeze) via the **amygdala** and **limbic system**
- Resources (oxygen and glucose) are **diverted from working memory** and the **prefrontal cortex** (the brain's executive center for reasoning, creativity, and collaboration) to the limbic system
- This impairs **analytical thinking, creative insight, and problem-solving**
- The threat response is **more intense and longer-lasting** than reward responses
- In its extreme form, this is called an **"amygdala hijack"** -- the threat-detection system takes over so completely that the rational brain goes offline; "you don't decide to panic -- your amygdala decides for you"
- We have very little time -- something on the order of **1/3 of a second** -- to "put on the brakes" and intervene to reappraise a situation (Ed Batista summary of Rock's work)

**The Reward Response Mechanism**:
- When a SCARF domain is **rewarded**, the brain enters a **toward state**, releasing neurotransmitters such as **dopamine** and **oxytocin**
- The **prefrontal cortex** is fully engaged for complex thinking, innovation, and learning
- People are willing to take interpersonal risks
- This is the brain state that **psychological safety enables**

**Critical Asymmetry**: The brain's threat circuits are **approximately five times more sensitive** than its reward circuits. As Rock notes, "There are five times as many threat circuits as reward circuits, so the brain is very easily tipped into threat mode." People move away from threats "more quickly and more vigorously" than toward rewards. This negativity bias evolved for survival but creates significant challenges in modern workplaces.

**Social Pain = Physical Pain**: The brain treats social threats as **equivalently dangerous to physical threats**. Naomi Eisenberger's landmark research at UCLA (2003) using the **Cyberball paradigm** showed that social exclusion activates the **dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC)** and **anterior insula** -- the same brain regions involved in processing physical pain. As Matthew Lieberman hypothesized, humans evolved this link because "being socially connected to caregivers is necessary for survival."

### The Five SCARF Domains in Detail

#### S -- Status: Relative Importance

**Definition**: Our perception of where we stand relative to others -- how competent, respected, and valued we feel. It is less about job title and more about "Do people here treat me like I matter?"

**Neuroscience**:
- Status is "the most significant determinant of human longevity and health" (citing Michael Marmot's research). High status correlates with longevity and better health outcomes
- Joan Chiao's research (2003) showed that status neural circuitry is similar to number processing -- the brain literally ranks people
- Keise Izuma's research (2008) demonstrated that **praise activates the same reward regions as financial windfalls**
- Even meaningless stakes can trigger status-related threat responses, suggesting small interpersonal dynamics matter enormously in organizational settings
- When status is threatened, **cortisol rises** and prefrontal cortex function diminishes

**Psychological Safety Connection**: When leaders publicly criticize, dismiss ideas, or create competitive ranking systems, they trigger status threats that silence people. When leaders **acknowledge contributions, ask for expertise, and create opportunities to shine**, they activate status rewards.

**Threat triggers**: Public criticism, being bypassed for input, unfavorable comparisons, stack ranking, being corrected in front of peers
**Reward triggers**: Positive feedback, being asked for expertise, public recognition, growth opportunities, learning new skills

#### C -- Certainty: Ability to Predict the Future

**Definition**: Our ability to predict what will happen next; the brain's craving for patterns and predictability. As Rock notes: "The brain craves a sense of understanding the future."

**Neuroscience**:
- Ambiguity registers as an **error** in the **anterior cingulate cortex**, creating a distracted, anxious state
- Lacking certainty forces the brain to "use dramatically more resources, involving the more energy-intensive prefrontal cortex, to process moment-to-moment experience" (Rock, *Managing with the Brain in Mind*)
- Even small amounts of certainty (like knowing a meeting agenda or timeline for decisions) significantly reduce threat activation

**Psychological Safety Connection**: When people don't know whether it's safe to speak up, the uncertainty itself is threatening. Clear norms, rituals, and predictable leader responses create certainty that enables risk-taking.

**SCARF 2025 Update**: Certainty fell from the **#1 driver in 2012** to the **very last position in 2025**. NLI suggests Certainty may have become a "hygiene factor" -- its absence still creates threat responses, but its presence no longer drives engagement. After years of global upheaval (pandemic, geopolitical instability), people may have developed a "resilience muscle" around uncertainty.

**Threat triggers**: Ambiguous expectations, unpredictable leader reactions, organizational restructuring, vague feedback
**Reward triggers**: Clear agendas, transparent decision-making, consistent responses to mistakes, explicit team norms

#### A -- Autonomy: Sense of Control Over Events

**Definition**: Our perception of having control over our environment and choices; the feeling of having options, agency, and self-direction.

**Neuroscience**:
- Autonomy functions as "an intrinsic reward that buffers against stress," particularly valuable as work complexity increases
- The loss of autonomy activates the same stress circuits as physical restraint
- Judith Rodin and Ellen Langer's landmark 1977 study showed that the perception of autonomy literally **extended the lifespans of nursing home residents**
- Autonomy is deeply intertwined with certainty -- having choices increases the perception of control and predictability

**Psychological Safety Connection**: Micromanagement is one of the fastest destroyers of both autonomy and psychological safety. When leaders tell people exactly what to do and how to do it, they trigger threat responses. When leaders set direction but empower choices about how to get there, they enable both autonomy and safety.

**SCARF 2025 Update**: Autonomy saw **the most dramatic increase in importance**, jumping from the bottom tier in 2012 to the **#2 position in 2025**. This shift likely reflects the rise of remote/hybrid work and growing expectations for workplace agency.

**Threat triggers**: Micromanagement, rigid processes, no voice in decisions, being told rather than asked
**Reward triggers**: Choice in methods, flexible work arrangements, self-directed goals, input into decisions

#### R -- Relatedness: Safety with Others

**Definition**: Whether we perceive others as friend or foe; our sense of belonging, trust, and social connection.

**Neuroscience**:
- Relatedness triggers **oxytocin secretion** when social connection strengthens, enabling trust and collaboration
- When people feel like outsiders, the brain activates the same circuits as physical pain
- The brain categorizes people as **"in-group" or "out-group" within milliseconds** -- this unconscious categorization profoundly affects trust, collaboration, and willingness to take interpersonal risks
- Loneliness activates threat systems -- social isolation is perceived by the brain as genuinely dangerous

**Psychological Safety Connection**: This is the SCARF domain most directly mapped to psychological safety. When people feel they belong and are accepted by the group, they experience relatedness reward. The "shared belief" that defines psychological safety IS fundamentally a relatedness phenomenon. Amy Edmondson's definition -- "the shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking" -- is, neurologically, a description of activated relatedness reward circuitry.

**Threat triggers**: Exclusion, cliques, lack of personal connection, feeling like an outsider, being ignored
**Reward triggers**: Shared experiences, personal check-ins, inclusive language, team rituals, vulnerability from leaders

#### F -- Fairness: Perception of Equitable Exchanges

**Definition**: Our perception of just and equitable exchanges between people.

**Neuroscience**:
- Perceived fairness **"activates reward circuitry similar to receiving a monetary reward"** (SCARF 2025 paper)
- Unfairness triggers the **anterior insula** -- "the same region associated with physical disgust" (SCARF 2025 paper)
- Matthew Lieberman and Golnaz Tabibnia's research showed that fairness produces **chocolate-like reward responses** in the brain
- People will sacrifice their own rewards to punish unfairness (as demonstrated in Ultimatum Game research) -- the fairness response is so strong it overrides economic self-interest

**Psychological Safety Connection**: When processes, promotions, recognition, or speaking opportunities are perceived as unfair, psychological safety collapses. People who feel the system is rigged will not take risks within it. Transparency and consistent standards are essential.

**SCARF 2025 Update**: Fairness **rose to the #1 position** in the 2025 data (up significantly from 2012). The shift to fairness as the dominant driver reflects heightened global awareness around equity, justice, and personal agency.

**Threat triggers**: Favoritism, opaque decisions, unequal speaking time, inconsistent standards, hidden agendas
**Reward triggers**: Transparent processes, equal airtime, meritocratic systems, open decision-making

### SCARF and Psychological Safety: The Direct Mapping

| SCARF Domain | When Threatened (Safety Decreases) | When Rewarded (Safety Increases) |
|---|---|---|
| **Status** | Public criticism, dismissal of ideas | Recognition of contributions, asking for expertise |
| **Certainty** | Unpredictable leader reactions to mistakes | Consistent, predictable responses; clear norms |
| **Autonomy** | Micromanagement, no voice in decisions | Empowerment, choice, input into process |
| **Relatedness** | Exclusion, feeling like an outsider | Belonging, personal connection, shared experiences |
| **Fairness** | Favoritism, opaque processes | Transparency, equal treatment, consistent standards |

**Key insight**: Psychological safety exists when all five SCARF domains are in a reward (toward) state simultaneously. A single SCARF threat can collapse the entire sense of safety because of the brain's **5:1 threat-to-reward sensitivity ratio**.

### SCARF in 2025: Major Research Update

In 2025, the NeuroLeadership Institute released its latest foundational paper: **"SCARF in 2025: Updating the Social Neuroscience of Collaborating with Others,"** authored by Brigid Lynn, PhD, MPH, Emma Sarro, PhD, and Dr. David Rock.

**The Dataset**: Over **15,000 SCARF assessment responses** collected between 2024 and 2025.

**The "Great Reversal"** -- Major findings:

| Domain | Ranking in 2012 | Ranking in 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fairness** | Lower tier | **#1** | Major increase |
| **Autonomy** | Bottom | **#2** | Most dramatic increase |
| **Status** | Mid-range | **#3** | Shifted |
| **Relatedness** | Top tier | **#4** | Declined |
| **Certainty** | **#1** | **#5 (Last)** | Dramatic decline |

**Gender Differences**: Females generally report **higher overall sensitivity to all SCARF domains** compared to males, with a particularly strong emphasis on **Status and Fairness**.

**Role/Seniority Differences**: As individuals move up the ladder into senior leadership, their sensitivity to Certainty tends to **decrease** while their need for Autonomy **increases**. This creates a dangerous **"blind spot"** where leaders inadvertently micro-manage or under-communicate, not realizing that their team members (especially individual contributors) have a much higher need for explicit Certainty and Fairness than they do.

**Implication for Leaders**: The 2025 data tells leaders they must prioritize **fairness and autonomy** above all, while recognizing that the SCARF domains are universal but their relative importance is **highly individualized** and varies by role, gender, and region.

## 3. David Rock's Specific Work on Psychological Safety

### The Core Neuroscience Argument

David Rock's contribution to the psychological safety literature is to provide **the neurological mechanism** explaining why psychological safety matters. Where Amy Edmondson (Harvard Business School) identified psychological safety as the key variable in team performance, Rock explains **what happens in the brain** when psychological safety is present or absent.

**When Psychological Safety Is Absent** (Threat State):
- The brain activates a **fight/flight/freeze response** via the amygdala and limbic system
- Blood, oxygen, and glucose are **redistributed from the brain to other parts of the body**, preparing for physical defense
- The **prefrontal cortex** -- responsible for creativity, complex reasoning, collaboration, and learning -- is **functionally impaired**
- People experience defensive emotions: blame, insecurity, hostility
- Neuroscience shows that in high-stress or socially threatening moments, "rational thought shuts down -- the amygdala overrides the prefrontal cortex"
- Everyday social threats -- being "ignored, dismissed, silenced, humiliated, embarrassed, rejected, cast out, isolated, ostracized, or excluded" -- are perceptually as painful and threatening as physical trauma

**When Psychological Safety Is Present** (Reward State):
- The prefrontal cortex is fully engaged
- Reward neurotransmitters (dopamine, oxytocin, serotonin) support curiosity, exploration, and connection
- The brain is neurologically capable of its highest cognitive functions
- People are willing to take interpersonal risks -- speaking up, admitting mistakes, asking questions, challenging the status quo

### Why People Stay Silent: The Neuroscience

Rock's work provides the neuroscience explanation for one of the most devastating organizational phenomena -- silence. People tend to stay silent rather than speak up because:

1. **The brain is wired for social survival**: Speaking up activates potential threats across multiple SCARF domains simultaneously -- Status (looking incompetent), Certainty (not knowing how people will react), Relatedness (risking exclusion), and Fairness (potential retaliation)
2. **Threat responses are faster and stronger than reward responses**: The brain processes potential social threats in milliseconds, before the prefrontal cortex can engage rational analysis
3. **The threat response is metabolically dominant**: There are approximately **five times as many threat circuits as reward circuits** in the brain
4. **Social pain lingers**: Unlike physical pain, which fades relatively quickly from memory, social pain can be **re-experienced at full intensity** simply by recalling the event
5. **Once bitten, twice shy**: The brain is designed to remember threats more vividly than rewards. A single experience of being punished for speaking up can create a lasting neural pattern of avoidance

As Rock summarizes: people "fear to face potential threat, embarrassment, rejection, or punishment, and tend to rather stay silent than to speak up, even if it could provide benefits for the team or organization."

### NLI's Three Habits of Psychological Safety

Working with Amy Edmondson, NLI developed a neuroscience-backed framework of **three habits** that create psychological safety:

#### Habit 1: Set the Stage
- Establishes clarity by creating a **shared foundation for work** and helping people understand they are part of a group
- Includes setting explicit expectations around **speaking up, disagreeing, and admitting mistakes**
- Neurologically: Creates **Certainty** (people know what is expected) and **Relatedness** (shared in-group identity)
- When people have a shared in-group identity, they feel a sense of relatedness to one another, and this relatedness provides a way to connect individual values with an overall purpose

#### Habit 2: Invite Participation
- Increases engagement by ensuring people **speak up and are heard**
- Leaders should **invite participation from others before speaking up themselves** -- this mitigates the power differential that creates Status and Autonomy threats
- Neurologically: Activates **Autonomy** (people have voice and choice) and **Status** (their contribution is valued)

#### Habit 3: Respond Thoughtfully
- Ensures people **continue** speaking up by rewarding desired behaviors
- Be warm and inviting, and also challenge when necessary -- but do it kindly
- Neurologically: Creates **Certainty** (predictable positive responses to vulnerability) and **Fairness** (consistent treatment regardless of the message)
- The goal is to make speaking up neurologically rewarding rather than threatening

### NLI's Conversation with Amy Edmondson: Key Insights

David Rock and Amy Edmondson co-developed critical insights about the interplay between psychological safety and accountability:

**Misconception 1: Psychological Safety = Feeling Good**
Edmondson clarifies: "It doesn't mean freedom to not do my job correctly." Psychological safety involves **healthy friction and respectful debate**.

**Misconception 2: Psychological Safety = Getting Your Way**
All voices are heard and considered, though not necessarily agreed with.

**Misconception 3: Psychological Safety vs. Accountability**
This is a false dichotomy. The framework identifies **four workplace zones**:

| | Low Accountability | High Accountability |
|---|---|---|
| **Low Safety** | Apathy Zone | Anxiety Zone |
| **High Safety** | Comfort Zone | **Learning Zone (Optimal)** |

The **Learning Zone** -- high safety combined with high accountability -- is where organizations achieve peak performance, innovation, and growth.

**Misconception 4: Accountability = Punishment**
Edmondson defines true accountability as **"psychological ownership"** -- an intrinsic commitment to excellence. Rock emphasizes that growth-oriented accountability should come from within rather than external surveillance, which actually diminishes internal motivation.

## 4. The "Toward" vs. "Away" State

### The Core Mechanism

David Rock identifies two fundamental brain states that determine human performance:

**Toward State** (Approach/Reward):
- Prefrontal cortex is fully engaged
- Capacity for complex thinking, creativity, collaboration, and learning is maximized
- Dopamine is released, enabling curiosity and exploration
- Oxytocin supports trust and connection
- People are willing to take interpersonal risks
- Innovation, problem-solving, and growth are possible
- **This is the brain state that psychological safety enables**

**Away State** (Avoidance/Threat):
- Amygdala and limbic system hijack cognitive resources
- Prefrontal cortex function is diminished -- up to **50% reduction in cognitive capacity** (Rock)
- Cortisol and adrenaline flood the brain, narrowing attention to the perceived threat
- People become defensive, risk-averse, and self-protective
- Learning, creativity, and collaboration become **physiologically impossible**
- **This is the brain state that psychological unsafety creates**

### The Accelerator vs. Brake Metaphor

Rock uses a powerful metaphor based on brain architecture:

- The strong emotions generated by the **limbic system** act as the brain's **"accelerator"**
- The opposing forces generated by the **ventrolateral prefrontal cortex** act as the brain's **"braking system"**
- **Critical asymmetry**: The resources available for acceleration (the capacity of the limbic system) are **far greater** than the resources available for braking (the capacity of the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex)
- This means threat responses are neurologically stronger than our capacity to regulate them
- We have approximately **1/3 of a second** to intervene and reappraise a situation before the threat response takes over completely

### Performance Implications

When leaders create psychologically unsafe environments, they are **literally reducing their team's cognitive capacity**. The brain cannot simultaneously be in a threat state and perform complex creative work. This is why psychologically unsafe teams underperform -- it is not a matter of motivation or effort, it is a matter of **neurobiology**.

Rock describes this in *Managing with the Brain in Mind*: When people feel threatened, the brain diverts resources from the prefrontal cortex to the limbic system. The very capabilities organizations need most -- **creativity, collaboration, complex problem-solving** -- are the first to go offline.

**Practical implications for organizations**:
- A psychologically unsafe meeting is a **cognitively impaired meeting** -- participants physically cannot think at their best
- Teams that experience chronic threat states develop **learned helplessness** -- the brain stops trying to engage its higher functions
- The toward/away state is not binary; it exists on a continuum, with even mild threats creating measurable cognitive impairment
- Recovery from a threat state takes significantly longer than it takes to create one -- organizational trust is slow to build and fast to destroy

### The 5:1 Ratio

Research in neuroscience and positive psychology suggests that it takes approximately **five positive interactions to offset one negative interaction** (related to Fredrickson's positivity ratio research). This means that a single SCARF threat -- one public criticism, one dismissive response -- requires multiple reward experiences to restore the toward state.

### Toward and Away Emotions

Rock classifies limbic system signals as **toward emotions** and **away emotions**:

**Toward emotions**: Curiosity, happiness, excitement, engagement, anticipation. When experienced, the limbic system provides a **boost of energy** and the prefrontal cortex remains fully engaged.

**Away emotions**: Anxiety, fear, sadness, anger, defensiveness. When experienced, **cortisol and adrenaline levels heighten**, cognitive resources are redirected to threat processing, and higher-order thinking diminishes.

## 5. Growth Mindset and Neuroscience

### David Rock's Integration with Carol Dweck's Work

The NeuroLeadership Institute gained deep expertise in growth mindset and has helped many organizations transform their cultures. NLI's work includes a direct Q&A with growth mindset's originator, psychologist Carol Dweck, presenting research-backed advice for leaders implementing growth mindset in their teams.

### Carol Dweck's Framework Through a Neuroscience Lens

David Rock's integration of Dweck's growth mindset with neuroscience reveals:

- **Fixed mindset** creates chronic **Status threat** -- every challenge is a potential exposure of inadequacy
- **Growth mindset** converts Status threats into **learning opportunities** -- mistakes become data, not failures
- Neuroscience shows the brain literally **processes errors differently** under growth vs. fixed mindset:
  - **Fixed mindset**: Error-related negativity (ERN) triggers avoidance responses; the brain treats mistakes as threats
  - **Growth mindset**: Error-related positivity (Pe) activates learning circuits; the brain treats mistakes as information

### Neuroplasticity as the Foundation

Carol Dweck built her growth mindset research on the theory of **neuroplasticity** -- the brain's ability to continue to form new connections into adulthood, after it has been damaged or when stimulated by new experiences. This is the biological basis for the growth mindset claim that abilities can be developed:

- The brain physically **rewires itself** based on experience and practice
- Neural pathways that are used frequently become stronger (myelination)
- New neurons can be generated throughout life (neurogenesis, particularly in the hippocampus)
- **Psychological safety is a prerequisite** for this neuroplasticity to be activated in organizational settings -- a brain in threat state prioritizes survival, not learning and growth

### The Growth Mindset + Psychological Safety Synergy

NLI's research demonstrates that growth mindset and psychological safety are **synergistic and mutually reinforcing**:

- **Growth mindset** reframes mistakes as learning opportunities (reducing Status threat from the SCARF model)
- **Psychological safety** enables people to actually admit mistakes (activating Relatedness reward)
- Together, they create a **learning culture** where failure drives improvement rather than punishment
- Adopting a growth mindset "fundamentally shifts how people experience feedback -- transforming it from being a stressful experience to a valuable opportunity for learning" (NLI)

### The Feedback Connection

Growth mindset changes the neuroscience of feedback:
- In a **fixed mindset**, feedback threatens Status ("You're saying I'm not good enough")
- In a **growth mindset**, feedback activates curiosity ("You're showing me how to get better")
- NLI's research shows that asking people to **request** feedback (rather than imposing it) can **halve stress levels** for everyone involved -- a direct application of Autonomy reward from the SCARF model
- Growth mindset provides the cognitive frame; psychological safety provides the environmental container; together, they make feedback neurologically safe

## 6. NLI's Organizational Applications

### The Three-Legged Stool Framework

NLI identifies three interdependent elements for high-performing organizations:

1. **Growth Mindset**: The conviction that "abilities can be improved over time; they aren't set from birth" and represent learnable capacities rather than fixed traits
2. **Psychological Safety**: "The confidence and assuredness to take interpersonal risks, speak up, or disagree openly without fear of punishment"
3. **Accountability**: "The commitment to -- and sense of ownership over -- one's responsibilities"

**Why All Three Are Required**: NLI's key insight: **"If you have more than three priorities, you actually have zero priorities."** Even among Fortune 100 companies, "it's extremely rare for even the most high-performing, high-powered executives to strike the right balance" between all three.

- **Without Growth Mindset**: People focus on appearing competent rather than improving; they avoid failure at all costs and resist collaborative learning
- **Without Psychological Safety**: Despite wanting to learn, people fear interpersonal risk; innovation stalls, resentment builds, potential remains untapped
- **Without Accountability**: Interpersonal dynamics improve but performance becomes secondary; results suffer because expectations lack clarity

### The SCARF Connection to the Three-Legged Stool

Each element maps to SCARF domains:
- **Growth Mindset** reduces Status threats (mistakes become learning, not incompetence)
- **Psychological Safety** activates Relatedness and Certainty rewards (belonging and predictability)
- **Accountability** creates Certainty through clear expectations and Fairness through consistent standards

### Microsoft Partnership: The Defining Case Study

NLI helped Microsoft transform its culture under CEO Satya Nadella, creating one of the most cited organizational transformation stories in modern business:

**The Challenge**: When Nadella took over as CEO in 2014, he inherited a company "fading toward irrelevance, plagued by internal fights and inertia." Microsoft had a deeply entrenched **"know-it-all"** culture where employees prioritized appearing smart over learning.

4. **Feedback Revolution**: Microsoft shifted from giving feedback to **asking for feedback** -- aligning with NLI's neuroscience research showing this reduces stress by approximately 50%.

### NLI's SEEDS Model for Bias

The **SEEDS Model** organizes more than **150 cognitive biases** into five broad, memorable categories:

1. **S**imilarity Bias: Preferring people who are like us (threatens Relatedness for out-group members)
2. **E**xpedience Bias: Jumping to conclusions quickly (threatens Fairness when decisions are hasty)
3. **E**xperience Bias: Assuming our perception is objective reality (threatens Status of those with different views). As NLI notes: "We may fail to remember that other people see the world differently, assuming our view of a situation constitutes the whole truth"
4. **D**istance Bias: Prioritizing what's closer in time or space (threatens Fairness for remote workers). "Biases become common in today's globalized world, emerging in meetings when folks in the room fail to gather input from remote colleagues"
5. **S**afety Bias: Overweighting threats relative to opportunities (amplifies away-state responses). "People protect against loss more than seeking out gain"

**Connection to Psychological Safety**: Each SEEDS bias, when left unchecked, erodes psychological safety by creating unfair, exclusionary, or dismissive environments. NLI developed **DECIDE** -- a groundbreaking application of the SEEDS model designed to build smarter teams by getting intentional about mitigating biases, finding blind spots, and eliminating groupthink.

## 7. The Neuroscience of Feedback, Inclusion, and Belonging

### Social Rejection and Physical Pain: The Research

The foundational neuroscience research demonstrating that social pain and physical pain share neural substrates comes from multiple landmark studies:

**Eisenberger, Lieberman, & Williams (2003)**: "Does Rejection Hurt?"
- Used the **Cyberball paradigm** -- participants played a virtual ball-tossing game, then were systematically excluded by other (simulated) players
- fMRI scans showed that social exclusion activated the **dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC)** -- the same brain region activated during physical pain
- Individuals who showed **greater activity in the dACC** reported **stronger feelings of social distress**
- Published in *Science*, this study fundamentally changed understanding of social pain

**Kross et al. (2011)**: "Social Rejection Shares Somatosensory Representations with Physical Pain"
- Published in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS)*
- Showed that intense social rejection activates the **secondary somatosensory cortex** and **dorsal posterior insula** -- regions involved in the sensory processing of physical pain
- This went beyond previous findings by showing overlap not just in affective pain regions but in sensory-discriminative pain regions

**Evolutionary Explanation**: Because of our prolonged period of developmental immaturity, the social attachment system may have **co-opted the pain system**, borrowing the pain signal to prevent the detrimental consequences of social separation. As Lieberman hypothesized: being socially connected to caregivers is necessary for survival, so the brain evolved to treat social disconnection as physically dangerous.

### David Rock's Application of This Research

Rock weaves this neuroscience into his practical leadership framework:

> "Our brain responds to social threats in [SCARF] dimensions just as it does to the threat of physical pain, a finding that has substantial implications for leadership practices."
> -- David Rock, *Managing with the Brain in Mind*

This means everyday workplace interactions carry the same neurological weight as survival threats:
- **Being excluded from a team discussion** literally activates pain circuits
- **Having an idea dismissed** triggers the same brain response as a physical blow
- **Being left out of information loops** is experienced as a physical threat
- The brain does not distinguish between social and physical danger

### The Neuroscience of Feedback

NLI's research on feedback has produced specific, actionable findings:

**The Problem**: Both giving and receiving feedback trigger **stress responses similar to facing a physical threat**. Research found that participants' heart rates jump as much as **50%** during feedback conversations -- equivalent spikes found during anxiety-producing tasks like public speaking.

**The Solution**: NLI research discovered that having a person **ask for feedback**, rather than receiving it unsolicited, **reduces stress response by approximately 50%** for both the giver and receiver. This simple shift transforms feedback from a threat into a choice, restoring **Autonomy** (the person chose to hear it) and making it more psychologically manageable.

**The Neuroscience Mechanism**: When we are not experiencing a threat response, we can better practice **mental contrasting** -- comparing how we're doing things now with a vision of how we could do better in the future. Threat responses shut down this capacity. By making feedback voluntary, the prefrontal cortex remains online and can actually process the information constructively.

**Practical Recommendation**: Lasting change starts with senior leaders who **model respectful feedback-seeking behavior**. Organizations like Microsoft have shifted from giving feedback to **asking for feedback** based on this neuroscience.

### Implications for Leaders: Every Interaction Signals Safety or Danger

Every interaction a leader has with their team either activates **belonging circuits** (oxytocin, dopamine, ventral vagal engagement) or **threat circuits** (cortisol, amygdala activation, sympathetic nervous system). There is no neutral interaction from the brain's perspective. Leaders are constantly, unconsciously, signaling safety or danger through:

- **Facial expressions**: The brain reads micro-expressions in milliseconds
- **Tone of voice**: Prosody (vocal tone, rhythm, emphasis) is processed before word content
- **Body language**: Posture signals dominance/submission and openness/closure
- **Response latency**: A delayed response to an email or message is interpreted as a threat signal
- **Attention quality**: Whether a leader is fully present or distracted during conversation

### The Neuroplasticity of Safety and Threat

The brain physically rewires itself based on repeated experience:

**In Psychologically Safe Environments**:
- **Neuroplasticity** enables learning and adaptation
- New neural pathways form for collaborative, creative, risk-taking behaviors
- Over time, psychologically safe behaviors become automatic (habit formation)
- The brain develops stronger reward circuitry for social engagement

**In Psychologically Unsafe Environments**:
- The brain wires for **hypervigilance and self-protection**
- Threat-detection circuits become **oversensitized** (lower threshold for triggering)
- The capacity for creative thinking and collaboration **physically atrophies** through disuse
- Avoidance behaviors become neurally encoded habits that are difficult to change

## 8. The AGES Model for Learning

NLI's **AGES Model** describes the four conditions required for the brain to encode new information effectively. The model is based on neuroscience research about hippocampal activation -- the hippocampus is the brain region active when new information is embedded into long-term memory. The hippocampus activates optimally when four conditions are met:

### A -- Attention
- Focused, undistracted attention (learners maintain focus for approximately **20 minutes** before requiring a mental refresh)
- Multitasking **significantly reduces** both memory retention and performance
- Strategy: "Build attention shifts every 20 minutes (e.g., changing speakers or chunking lessons)" and eliminate distractions

### G -- Generation
- Actively **creating connections** between new ideas and existing knowledge increases memory retention compared to just reading or hearing new information
- The practical application involves guiding participants to apply concepts to real-world situations, which strengthens neural encoding
- Strategy: Ask learners to generate their own insights rather than passively consuming content

### E -- Emotion
- "Schemas are built more strongly when **subtle positive emotion** is present in the learning process" while negative or intense emotions hinder acquisition
- **Psychological safety** directly enables the emotional conditions for learning -- anxiety and fear suppress hippocampal function, while mild positive emotions enhance it
- Strategy: Create learning environments that feel safe, curious, and engaging rather than high-stakes or evaluative

### S -- Spacing
- "Returning to the material after some time has passed is **key for learning retention**"
- Incorporating sleep intervals between sessions and selecting **multiple shorter sessions** over single extended sessions optimizes long-term memory consolidation
- Strategy: Space learning over days and weeks, not hours

### Connection to Psychological Safety and Nudge Design

The AGES Model directly informs how nudges for executives should be designed:
- **Attention**: Nudges must be brief (15-30 words) to capture attention within short windows
- **Generation**: Nudges should prompt leaders to connect the concept to their own experience
- **Emotion**: Nudges should create mild positive emotion (curiosity, not anxiety)
- **Spacing**: Nudges should arrive at regular intervals (2-3 per week) for habit formation

## 10. Recent NLI Research and Developments (2024-2026)

### SCARF 2025: The Great Reversal

The most significant recent development is the **SCARF 2025 update**, based on 15,000+ assessment responses, showing a complete reversal in what drives people at work compared to 2012 data. Fairness and Autonomy have emerged as the top drivers, while Certainty -- previously #1 -- has fallen to last place.

### Psychological Safety as Enterprise Priority (2025)

NLI's 2024-2025 content shows that the topics capturing leaders' attention include **accountability, psychological safety, learning and development, and understanding leadership**. NLI positions three pillars as supporting modern leadership: **growth mindset, accountability, and psychological safety**, where leaders need to foster cultures where mistakes are seen as chances to improve and coworkers feel safe to speak up.

### Psychological Safety and AI (2025)

NLI research emphasizes that psychological safety is becoming **even more critical** as organizations navigate AI adoption:
- Leaders need to create environments where people feel safe to **experiment with AI tools**, admit confusion, and raise concerns about AI-driven decisions
- The fear of AI replacing jobs threatens all five SCARF domains simultaneously (Status, Certainty, Autonomy, Relatedness, Fairness)
- Organizations that lack psychological safety will struggle to adopt AI effectively

### Summit 2025: "Thrive Through Complexity"

The NeuroLeadership Summit 2025 focuses on how neuroscience can help organizations and leaders thrive through increasing complexity -- including AI integration, hybrid work, and rapid organizational change.

### Common Mistakes Organizations Make with Psychological Safety (2025)

NLI identified four critical mistakes:

1. **Confusing psychological safety with niceness**: Teams with true psychological safety are not afraid to engage in robust, healthy debate
2. **Thinking everyone gets their way**: All voices are heard and considered, though not necessarily agreed with
3. **Conflating care with psychological safety**: During the pandemic, empathy and kindness were conflated with psychological safety -- though they are separate concepts
4. **Separating accountability from psychological safety**: The false dichotomy of psychological safety versus accountability comes from a misunderstanding; they work hand in hand

### Neuroscience-Informed Nudge Design (2025-2026)

NLI's approach to behavioral nudges integrates SCARF awareness:
- Nudges should **protect Status** (frame as competitive advantage, not remediation)
- Nudges should **increase Certainty** (predictable delivery, clear expected behavior)
- Nudges should **maximize Autonomy** (offer choices, not directives)
- Nudges should **strengthen Relatedness** (peer cohort data, shared challenges)
- Nudges should **ensure Fairness** (transparent methodology, consistent delivery)
