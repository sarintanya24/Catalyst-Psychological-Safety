## 1. What Is Psychological Safety?

### 1.1 The Formal Definition

Psychological safety is defined as **"a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking."** This is the foundational definition established by Amy C. Edmondson, Novartis Professor of Leadership and Management at Harvard Business School, in her landmark 1999 paper published in *Administrative Science Quarterly*.

More specifically, Edmondson described it as:

> "A team climate characterized by interpersonal trust and mutual respect in which people are comfortable being themselves."
>
> -- Amy C. Edmondson, "Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams" (1999)

In practical terms, when a team possesses psychological safety, members feel able to:
- Ask for help without being seen as incompetent
- Admit mistakes without fear of punishment
- Raise concerns without being labeled as negative
- Challenge ideas (including those of authority figures) without retaliation
- Offer new suggestions without being ridiculed

### 1.2 Academic Foundations and Historical Origins

The concept of psychological safety did not originate with Edmondson. Its intellectual lineage extends back decades:

**Carl Rogers (1954):** The humanistic psychologist Carl Rogers was the first to use the term "psychological safety" in 1954, in the context of fostering creativity. He proposed that psychological safety and psychological freedom were necessary conditions for creative expression.

**Edgar Schein and Warren Bennis (1965):** The concept was introduced into the field of management studies by MIT professors Edgar Schein and Warren Bennis in their 1965 book *Personal and Organizational Change Through Group Methods: The Laboratory Approach*. They defined it as:

> "An atmosphere where one can take chances (which experimentalism implies) without fear and with sufficient protection... thus a climate is built which encourages provisional tries and which tolerates failure without retaliation, renunciation, or guilt."
>
> -- Schein & Bennis (1965)

Schein and Bennis built on Kurt Lewin's three-stage model of organizational learning and change (unfreezing, changing, refreezing), suggesting that psychologically safe work environments facilitate the "unfreezing" process where employees become receptive to changes to the status quo.

**William Kahn (1990):** Boston University professor William Kahn conducted a pivotal study, published as "Psychological Conditions of Personal Engagement and Disengagement at Work" in the *Academy of Management Journal* (Vol. 33, pp. 692-724). Kahn identified three psychological conditions necessary for employee engagement:

1. **Meaningfulness** -- a sense of purpose behind the work
2. **Psychological Safety** -- "the sense of being able to show and employ self without fear of negative consequences to self-image, status, or career"
3. **Psychological Availability** -- "the sense of having the physical, emotional, or psychological resources to personally engage at a particular moment"

Kahn's research, based on qualitative studies of summer camp counselors and architecture firm employees, established psychological safety as a prerequisite for authentic self-expression and full engagement at work.

**Amy Edmondson (1999):** Edmondson's contribution was to elevate psychological safety from an individual-level concept to a **team-level construct**, demonstrating empirically how it functions as a shared group belief that drives team learning behavior and, ultimately, team performance. Her 1999 study of 51 work teams in a manufacturing company showed that team psychological safety is associated with learning behavior, and that learning behavior mediates the relationship between team psychological safety and team performance.

### 1.3 How It Is Defined in Organizational Psychology

In contemporary organizational psychology, psychological safety is understood as:

- **A group-level phenomenon:** It is not merely an individual feeling but a shared perception among team members about the social norms governing interpersonal interaction within the group.
- **A climate for interpersonal risk-taking:** The core of the construct is about whether people believe they can take interpersonal risks -- speaking up, being vulnerable, admitting uncertainty -- without suffering negative consequences.
- **A belief, not a personality trait:** It describes a property of the work environment, not an individual's disposition. The same person can experience high psychological safety in one team and low psychological safety in another.
- **Dynamic and emergent:** Recent research (Marlow, Lacerenza, & Salas, 2025) examines how psychological safety consensus emerges over time within teams, finding that it is higher for newcomers and subsequently decreases as individuals become more tenured -- suggesting it is not static but evolves with group experience.

## 2. The Four Stages of Psychological Safety

### 2.1 Timothy R. Clark's Model

Dr. Timothy R. Clark, founder and CEO of LeaderFactor and an international authority on psychological safety and innovation, developed a progressive four-stage model presented in his book *The 4 Stages of Psychological Safety: Defining the Path to Inclusion and Innovation* (Berrett-Koehler Publishers, 2020).

Clark's central thesis is that psychological safety is a **progression**, not a binary state. Teams move through four cumulative stages, each corresponding to a fundamental human need. The framework rests on two core dimensions:

- **Respect:** Acknowledging people's inherent worth and humanity
- **Permission:** Granting people the latitude to participate, learn, contribute, and challenge

Clark's essential insight is that **"we're humans first and employees second,"** and organizations must honor that reality to unlock performance.

### 2.2 The Four Stages in Detail

#### Stage 1: Inclusion Safety

- **Core Question:** "Can I be my authentic self?"
- **Human Need Satisfied:** The need to connect and belong
- **Definition:** Inclusion safety is the foundational stage where individuals feel a sense of belonging and acceptance within their teams and organizations. Members feel safe to be present, are not excluded, and feel wanted and appreciated.
- **Key Principle:** Worth precedes worthiness -- being human and harmless is sufficient qualification for belonging. No one should have to earn the right to be included.
- **What It Looks Like:** People feel comfortable being themselves. Differences in background, identity, and perspective are accepted rather than penalized.
- **When It Fails:** People mask their true identity, self-censor, or disengage entirely from the group.

#### Stage 2: Learner Safety

- **Core Question:** "Can I grow?"
- **Human Need Satisfied:** The need to learn and develop
- **Definition:** Learner safety creates an environment where individuals feel safe to learn, grow, and make mistakes without fear of embarrassment or punishment. People are encouraged to take risks, experiment, and ask questions.
- **Key Principle:** Fear is detached from mistakes; errors become rewarded learning opportunities rather than punishable offenses.
- **What It Looks Like:** Team members ask questions freely, experiment with new approaches, admit when they don't know something, and seek feedback without anxiety.
- **When It Fails:** People pretend to know things they don't, avoid asking questions, and hide mistakes rather than learning from them.

#### Stage 3: Contributor Safety

- **Core Question:** "Can I create value?"
- **Human Need Satisfied:** The need to make meaningful contributions
- **Definition:** Contributor safety means members feel safe to contribute their own ideas without fear of embarrassment or ridicule. This stage involves increased psychosocial vulnerability because volunteering original ideas exposes individuals to greater interpersonal risk.
- **Social Exchange:** Leaders provide autonomy, guidance, and encouragement in exchange for effort and results.
- **What It Looks Like:** People proactively share ideas, offer suggestions, and apply their unique skills and perspectives to solve problems.
- **When It Fails:** People withhold ideas, do the minimum required, and defer to authority even when they have better alternatives.

#### Stage 4: Challenger Safety

- **Core Question:** "Can I be candid about change?"
- **Human Need Satisfied:** The need to improve things
- **Definition:** Challenger safety represents the highest level of psychological safety. Individuals feel safe challenging the status quo, questioning authority, expressing dissenting opinions, and proposing significant changes without fear of punishment or retaliation.
- **Social Exchange:** Leaders provide protection ("air cover") in exchange for candor and constructive challenge.
- **What It Looks Like:** People openly question existing processes, push back on decisions they disagree with (including from leadership), and advocate for innovation and change.
- **When It Fails:** Organizations stagnate, groupthink prevails, and problems remain hidden until they become crises.

### 2.3 The Cumulative Nature of the Stages

A critical element of Clark's model is that the four stages are **cumulative and sequential**. Employees must feel safe at each previous stage before they can authentically progress to the next:

- You cannot contribute ideas (Stage 3) if you don't feel safe learning and asking questions (Stage 2).
- You cannot learn openly (Stage 2) if you don't feel you belong (Stage 1).
- You certainly cannot challenge the status quo (Stage 4) without all three preceding stages in place.

As Clark states, psychological safety means **"it's not expensive to interact socially in a team or an organization."** When the social cost of participation is high, people self-censor. The four stages provide a roadmap for systematically reducing that cost.

## 3. Amy Edmondson's Framework

### 3.1 The Origin Story: An "Intelligent Failure" in a Hospital

Amy Edmondson's discovery of psychological safety is itself a compelling story of scientific serendipity. As a doctoral student at Harvard Business School, Edmondson set out to study the relationship between teamwork quality and medical error rates in hospital nursing units. Her hypothesis was straightforward: **better teams should make fewer errors**.

The data told the opposite story. Teams that scored higher on her teamwork measures actually had **higher detected error rates**. Edmondson has described this as an "intelligent failure" that forced her to reconsider her assumptions.

The breakthrough came when she re-examined a survey item she had included: **"If you make a mistake in this unit, it won't be held against you."** This item correlated strongly with detected error rates -- but in the unexpected direction. The more people believed mistakes wouldn't be punished, the higher the reported errors.

Her research assistant, Andy Molinsky, conducted direct observations across the hospital units and found stark differences in culture. In some units, nurses described a **"non-punitive environment"** as essential to good patient care. In others, making mistakes meant **"you get in trouble"** or being put **"on trial."**

Molinsky's ranking of teams from most to least "open" nearly perfectly matched the error-rate data, leading Edmondson to the pivotal realization:

> "Better teams probably don't make more mistakes, but they are more able to discuss mistakes."
>
> -- Amy C. Edmondson

The "better" teams were not making more errors; they were **reporting more errors** because the interpersonal climate made it safe to do so. This insight -- that psychological safety enables learning through candid reporting -- became the foundation of her career's work.

### 3.2 The 1999 Study: "Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams"

Published in *Administrative Science Quarterly* (Vol. 44, No. 2, pp. 350-383), this study examined 51 work teams in a manufacturing company. Key findings included:

- Team psychological safety is positively associated with team learning behavior.
- Team learning behavior mediates the relationship between psychological safety and team performance.
- Psychological safety is a **group-level construct** -- a shared belief, not an individual trait.

The study introduced Edmondson's **seven-item psychological safety scale**, which remains the most widely used measure of team psychological safety:

1. If I make a mistake in this team, it is held against me. *(reverse scored)*
2. Members of this team are able to bring up problems and tough issues.
3. People on this team sometimes reject others for being different. *(reverse scored)*
4. It is safe to take a risk in this team.
5. It is difficult to ask other members of this team for help. *(reverse scored)*
6. No one on this team would deliberately act in a way that undermines my efforts.
7. Working with members of this team, my unique skills and talents are valued and utilized.

Each item is measured on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Items 1, 3, and 5 are reverse-scored. The deliberate mix of positively and negatively worded statements mitigates response bias.

### 3.3 The TEDx Talk: "Building a Psychologically Safe Workplace"

Edmondson's 2014 TEDx talk at Harvard Graduate School of Education distilled her research into an accessible framework. Key insights from the talk include:

**The Impression Management Problem:**
Edmondson identified the universal human tendency toward impression management as the primary barrier to psychological safety:

> "No one wakes up in the morning and jumps out of bed because they can't wait to get to work today to look ignorant, incompetent, intrusive, or negative."

She outlined how this manifests in four specific self-censoring behaviors:
- To avoid looking **ignorant**, people don't ask questions.
- To avoid looking **incompetent**, people don't admit weaknesses or mistakes.
- To avoid looking **intrusive**, people don't offer ideas.
- To avoid looking **negative**, people don't criticize the status quo.

**When Psychological Safety Matters Most:**
Edmondson emphasized that psychological safety is most critical in conditions of **uncertainty and interdependence** -- precisely the conditions that characterize most modern knowledge work. When work is certain and people are independent, psychological safety is a "nice-to-have." When outcomes are uncertain and people depend on each other, it becomes essential.

**The Definition as Expectation:**
In the talk, Edmondson refined her practical definition:

> "Psychological safety is a belief that it's absolutely okay -- in fact, it's expected -- to speak up with concerns, with questions, with ideas, with mistakes."

### 3.4 "The Fearless Organization" (2018)

Published by Wiley, *The Fearless Organization: Creating Psychological Safety in the Workplace for Learning, Innovation, and Growth* synthesized 20 years of Edmondson's research into a practical guide for leaders. The book's core argument is that in knowledge-intensive industries, the organizations that will thrive are those where people feel free to contribute their ideas, concerns, and questions.

### 3.5 The Learning Zone Framework (2x2 Matrix)

Perhaps Edmondson's most influential conceptual contribution beyond the definition itself is her **2x2 framework** mapping two independent dimensions:

- **Y-axis: Psychological Safety** (low to high)
- **X-axis: Motivation and Accountability / Performance Standards** (low to high)

This produces four distinct zones:

| | **Low Accountability** | **High Accountability** |
|---|---|---|
| **High Psychological Safety** | **Comfort Zone** | **Learning / High-Performance Zone** |
| **Low Psychological Safety** | **Apathy Zone** | **Anxiety Zone** |

#### The Four Zones Explained:

**Apathy Zone (Low Safety, Low Accountability):**
When both psychological safety and accountability are low, people disengage entirely. There is no motivation to perform and no safety net for speaking up. People "quietly quit," putting forth minimal effort. This is the worst-case scenario for any organization.

**Comfort Zone (High Safety, Low Accountability):**
When psychological safety is high but accountability is low, people feel safe and comfortable but lack the drive to push themselves. Teams become complacent. People enjoy working together but don't necessarily produce excellent results. This zone is often mistakenly what critics fear psychological safety creates.

**Anxiety Zone (Low Safety, High Accountability):**
When accountability is high but psychological safety is low, people are driven by fear. They are motivated to perform but afraid to ask for help, admit mistakes, or raise concerns. This leads to high stress, poor learning, hidden problems, and eventually catastrophic failures. Many high-pressure organizations operate in this zone.

**Learning / High-Performance Zone (High Safety, High Accountability):**
When both dimensions are high, teams achieve optimal performance. There is a sense of ownership and commitment to high standards, paired with the freedom to speak up, ask for help, share differing opinions, and learn from failure. This is where innovation, growth, and sustained high performance occur.

#### The Critical Insight:

The 2x2 matrix addresses one of the most common objections to psychological safety: the fear that creating it will lower standards. Edmondson's framework demonstrates that **psychological safety and accountability are not tradeoffs but complementary dimensions**. The goal is not to choose one or the other but to maximize both simultaneously.

### 3.6 Edmondson's Three Leadership Actions for Building Psychological Safety

In *The Fearless Organization* and subsequent work, Edmondson identifies three categories of leader behavior that build psychological safety:

1. **Frame the work:** Set the stage by establishing shared expectations about uncertainty, interdependence, and the need for everyone's voice. Acknowledge that failure and uncertainty are inherent in the work.

2. **Invite participation:** Proactively ask questions. Practice inquiry rather than advocacy. Use phrases like "What am I missing?" and "I need to hear from you." Create structured opportunities for input.

3. **Respond productively:** When people do speak up, respond with appreciation, destigmatize failure, and address clear violations with accountability. Thank people for raising concerns, even uncomfortable ones.

## 4. How Psychological Safety Differs From Related Concepts

### 4.1 Psychological Safety vs. Trust

Although trust and psychological safety share common ground, they are **distinct constructs** operating at different levels of analysis:

| Dimension | Trust | Psychological Safety |
|---|---|---|
| **Level of analysis** | Dyadic (between two individuals) | Group/team-level (shared belief) |
| **Focus** | Beliefs about a specific person | Beliefs about group norms |
| **Question answered** | "Can I rely on this person?" | "Can our team take interpersonal risks?" |
| **Direction** | A gives trust to B | Experienced collectively |

As Edmondson has clarified, trust pertains to whether someone believes another person **can and will do the right thing**, while psychological safety relates to whether others **will give you the benefit of the doubt** when you have asked for help or admitted a mistake.

A useful distinction: You can deeply trust an individual colleague yet still lack psychological safety within the team if the broader group norms punish vulnerability. Trust is a *foundational component* of psychological safety but is insufficient on its own -- psychological safety requires trust to be demonstrated **consistently across the entire group**.

Two types of trust are relevant:
- **Cognitive Trust:** Confidence in someone's competence and reliability
- **Affective Trust:** Belief in someone's capacity for caring, empathy, and integrity

Both contribute to but do not fully constitute psychological safety, which is an emergent property of the group, not the sum of bilateral trust relationships.

### 4.2 Psychological Safety vs. Psychological Comfort

This is perhaps the most dangerous conflation. Edmondson has been explicit:

> "Psychological safety does not imply ease or comfort; in contrast, it is about candor and willingness to engage in productive conflict so as to learn from different points of view."

Key distinctions:

- **Comfort** means the absence of discomfort, friction, or challenge.
- **Psychological safety** means the presence of interpersonal trust sufficient to enable discomfort, friction, and challenge.
- A psychologically safe environment is one where people feel safe enough **to be uncomfortable** -- to have hard conversations, to deliver difficult feedback, to disagree openly.
- "Wrapping each other in bubble wrap to avoid stepping on egos or power dynamics is the **opposite** of psychological safety."

Learning and growth inherently involve discomfort. Psychological safety does not remove that discomfort but rather creates the conditions in which people are willing to endure it.

### 4.3 Psychological Safety vs. Being Nice

A 2025 Harvard Business Review article ("What People Get Wrong About Psychological Safety") directly addresses this misconception:

> "Nice is the easy way out of a difficult conversation. Kind is being respectful, caring, and honest."

Edmondson herself has written:

> "Psychological safety isn't about being nice -- working in a psychologically safe environment doesn't mean that people always agree with one another for the sake of being kind. It also doesn't mean that people offer unequivocal praise or unconditional support for everything you have to say."

The "niceness" trap manifests in organizations where people are polite in meetings but disagree in private hallway conversations. This surface-level civility actually **undermines** psychological safety because it prevents the candid exchange of ideas that psychological safety is meant to enable.

Psychological safety is about **candor**, not courtesy. It makes possible:
- Productive disagreement
- Honest feedback
- Constructive challenge
- Free exchange of ideas

Being nice and being psychologically safe can coexist, but niceness alone is insufficient and can even be counterproductive when it suppresses truth-telling.

### 4.4 Psychological Safety vs. Lack of Accountability

This is the misconception that most concerns managers. The fear is: *"If I make people feel safe, they won't work hard."*

Edmondson's 2x2 matrix (detailed in Section 3.5) directly refutes this. Her research demonstrates that:

- Psychological safety without accountability leads to the **Comfort Zone** (complacency, low performance).
- Accountability without psychological safety leads to the **Anxiety Zone** (fear, hidden problems, burnout).
- High psychological safety combined with high accountability leads to the **Learning/High-Performance Zone**.

As stated on Edmondson's own website:

> "Psychological Safety does not equal 'anything goes.'"

Managers do not need to choose between psychological safety and accountability. They need both. Psychological safety enables accountability to function effectively because people can acknowledge shortfalls, discuss problems openly, and course-correct without fear.

### 4.5 Summary of What Psychological Safety Is NOT

According to the 2025 HBR research, six key misconceptions undermine psychological safety:

1. **It does not mean being nice.** It means being candid, respectful, and honest.
2. **It does not mean getting your way.** Being heard does not mean being agreed with.
3. **It does not mean job security.** It does not protect from business decisions like layoffs.
4. **It does not require trading off performance.** Safety and standards are complementary.
5. **It is not a policy.** It cannot be mandated; it is built "interaction by interaction."
6. **It does not require only top-down leadership.** It is "built by everyone -- at all levels of the company."

## 5. Key Academic Research and Evidence

### 5.1 Foundational Studies

#### Edmondson (1999) -- The Landmark Study
- **Publication:** "Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams," *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 44(2), 350-383.
- **Sample:** 51 work teams in a manufacturing company.
- **Key Finding:** Team psychological safety predicts team learning behavior, which in turn predicts team performance. Psychological safety operates as a shared group-level belief.
- **Impact:** This paper has been cited over 13,000 times and established psychological safety as a major construct in organizational behavior research.

#### Edmondson (1996) -- The Hospital Study
- **Publication:** "Learning from Mistakes is Easier Said Than Done," *Journal of Applied Behavioral Science*.
- **Sample:** Hospital nursing units studying medication error reporting.
- **Key Finding:** Units with higher psychological safety reported more errors -- not because they made more mistakes, but because the interpersonal climate made it safe to report them openly. Units with low psychological safety concealed errors, leading to repeated failures.

#### Kahn (1990) -- Engagement Conditions
- **Publication:** "Psychological Conditions of Personal Engagement and Disengagement at Work," *Academy of Management Journal*, 33(4), 692-724.
- **Key Finding:** Identified psychological safety as one of three conditions (alongside meaningfulness and availability) necessary for full employee engagement.

### 5.2 Major Meta-Analyses

#### Frazier, Fainshmidt, Klinger, Pezeshkan, & Vracheva (2017)
- **Publication:** "Psychological Safety: A Meta-Analytic Review and Extension," *Personnel Psychology*, 70, 113-165.
- **Scale:** Drew on **136 independent samples** representing over **22,000 individuals** and nearly **5,000 groups**.
- **Key Findings:**
  - Psychological safety is positively correlated with **creativity, job performance, and employee engagement**.
  - Psychological safety predicts both **task performance** and **organizational citizenship behaviors** over and above related concepts such as positive leader relations and work engagement.
  - Positive leader relations emerged as the strongest antecedent of psychological safety.
  - The meta-analysis confirmed that psychological safety has **incremental validity** beyond related constructs -- it is not merely a proxy for good leadership or team trust.

#### Meta-Analysis on Innovation Behavior (2022)
- **Publication:** "The Effect of Psychological Safety on Innovation Behavior: A Meta-Analysis," *ResearchGate*.
- **Key Finding:** A meta-analysis of **85 studies** found that psychological safety increases **idea generation by 25%** and **team performance by 30%**.

#### Meta-Analysis and Systematic Review of Measures (2024)
- **Publication:** Posted as a preprint on *medRxiv* in February 2024.
- **Focus:** Evaluated the quality of all existing measures used to assess psychological safety, assessing overall reliability and validity.

### 5.3 Google's Project Aristotle (2012-2015)

One of the most widely cited pieces of evidence for the importance of psychological safety comes from Google's internal research initiative, Project Aristotle:

- **Objective:** Understand what makes teams effective at Google.
- **Scale:** Studied over **180 teams** across Google, analyzing hundreds of variables.
- **Primary Finding:** **Psychological safety was the single most important factor** predicting team effectiveness -- more important than team composition, structure, or individual talent.
- **The Five Key Dynamics (in order of importance):**
  1. Psychological Safety
  2. Dependability
  3. Structure and Clarity
  4. Meaning
  5. Impact
- **Business Impact:** Sales teams with high psychological safety exceeded their sales targets by **17%**. Teams with low psychological safety fell short by up to **19%** -- a total **36-percentage-point spread** in revenue performance.

### 5.4 APA 2024 Work in America Survey

The American Psychological Association's 2024 Work in America Survey, conducted by The Harris Poll with more than **2,000 employed adults**, provided large-scale evidence on the prevalence and impact of psychological safety:

- **43%** of workers say they typically feel tense or stressed during their workday.
- That number rises to **61%** among workers experiencing lower psychological safety -- a **42% increase**.
- Workers with higher psychological safety were **10 times less likely** to describe their workplace as toxic compared to those with lower psychological safety.
- **15%** of workers reported experiencing a somewhat or very toxic workplace overall.
- **Two-thirds** of workers with cognitive, emotional, learning, or mental disabilities (66%) and physical disabilities (63%) reported lower levels of psychological safety, compared with **45%** of workers without disabilities.
- **41%** of workers worry that AI may make some or all of their job duties obsolete, highlighting new threats to psychological safety.

### 5.5 Quantified Organizational Impact (Multiple Studies)

Aggregating across studies and industry reports (2017-2025):

**Performance and Productivity:**
- **12% increase** in productivity when psychological safety improves (Gallup, 2017)
- **76% more engagement** on teams with high psychological safety (Peaky/multiple sources)
- **230% average ROI** per dollar invested in psychological safety initiatives (Psychology Today)
- **93%** of business leaders agree psychological safety boosts productivity and innovation (Workplace Options)

**Retention and Turnover:**
- **27% reduction** in turnover potential (Gallup, 2017)
- Only **3%** of employees plan to quit when psychological safety is high vs. **12%** when low (BCG Global, 2024) -- a **4x difference**
- One in four organizations identifies psychological safety as a top driver of retention (Predictive Index, 2022)

**Safety and Error Reduction:**
- **40% reduction** in safety incidents when psychological safety is present (Gallup, 2017)

**Burnout:**
- **60%** of low-resilience, low-safety employees feel burned out; **34%** consider quitting (meQuilibrium, 2022)
- Only **5%** of highly resilient, psychologically safe employees report burnout; **3%** consider quitting -- a **12x reduction in burnout and 11x reduction in quit intent**

**Leadership Impact:**
- When leader skills rated 9-10 by employees: average psychological safety score of **84%** (Ecsell Institute, 2022)
- When leader skills rated 6 or lower: average psychological safety score of just **36%** (Ecsell Institute, 2022)
- Only **26%** of leaders actively foster psychological safety in their teams (Journal of Accountancy)

### 5.6 Recent Research (2024-2026)

#### Psychological Safety and Patient Safety (2024)
A systematic and narrative review published in *PMC* (2024) investigated the quantitative evidence linking psychological safety to objective patient safety outcomes. The review searched databases through February 2024 and found significant positive relationships between team psychological safety and patient safety indicators in healthcare settings.

#### Psychological Safety and Innovation (2024)
A 2024 study published in *PLOS ONE* ("The Impact of Team Psychological Safety on Employee Innovative Performance") found that the three dimensions of team psychological safety -- team collaboration and understanding, team information sharing, and team give-and-take balance -- each had a **significant positive impact on employee innovative performance**, with communication behavior serving as a mediator variable.

#### Antecedents in High-Risk Occupations (2025)
A scoping review published in the *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* (2025) synthesized literature from 2010-2025 on psychological safety antecedents in healthcare and public safety professions. Key finding: **transformational and inclusive leaders who admit mistakes and ask for input create the strongest psychological safety**, particularly critical in high-risk environments.

#### Temporal Dynamics of Psychological Safety (2025)
Research by Marlow, Lacerenza, and Salas, published in *Small Group Research* (2025), examined how psychological safety consensus emerges over time. A notable finding: **psychological safety was higher for newcomers** in an organization and subsequently **decreased as individuals became more tenured** -- challenging the assumption that psychological safety simply builds over time.

#### The Curvilinear Effect (2023-2024)
Research published in the *Journal of Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* (2023) found that high psychological safety correlates with better performance up to the **80th percentile**; beyond that, performance **declines**. This suggests there may be an optimal level of psychological safety, and that extreme levels could potentially reduce performance pressure or enable excessive comfort.

### 5.7 The Implementation Gap

Despite overwhelming evidence for its importance, the data reveal a significant gap between recognition and action:

- **63%** of workers don't feel safe sharing opinions; **60%** say they can't be themselves at work (Mental Health America, 2024)
- **45%** of American workers feel unsafe sharing opinions due to fear of negative consequences (Ipsos)
- **84%** of workers consider psychological safety one of three most valued workplace aspects (Oyster HR, 2023)
- **89%** of employees say psychological safety is essential (multiple industry surveys)
- Yet only **26%** of leaders actively foster it in their teams

This gap -- between what workers need, what research validates, and what leaders actually do -- represents one of the most significant opportunities for organizational improvement today.
